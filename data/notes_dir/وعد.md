Yes. There is a very close **WAD-/WAT-/VAÐ-** word-family across Old English, Old High German, and Old Norse.

| Language | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Old English** | **wadan** | to go, advance, travel, proceed |
| **Old Norse** | **vaða** | to go, wade, pass through |
| **Old High German** | **watan** | to go, pass, wade |
| Old Frisian | **wada** | to go, pass, wade |
| Modern English | **wade** | move through water/mud; originally more generally “go/proceed” |
| Modern German | **waten** | to wade |

These are treated as cognates from Proto-Germanic **\*wadaną**, basically **“to go, proceed, pass through.”** :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

The especially interesting point for your spelling is that **WADA** itself is essentially attested in the Germanic family: Old Frisian **wada**, alongside Old English **wadan**. Old Norse underwent the regular Germanic sound/spelling development giving **vaða**, where **ð** is the “th” sound in *this*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

So you can line them up approximately:

**WADA → WADAN → WATAN → VAÐA → WADE**

The oldest meaning was broader than modern English *wade*: **“to go, move forward, advance, pass through.”** The narrower sense “walk through water” developed from that. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}
