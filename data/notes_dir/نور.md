The English word **Norse** ultimately means **“northern” / “from the north,” especially Norwegian or Scandinavian**.

The interesting part is that **English “Norse” was not directly borrowed from Old Norse**. English got it around the 1590s from obsolete Dutch **Noorsch**, meaning “Norwegian,” shortened from **noordsch**, literally **“northern.”** :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

The underlying Germanic family looks like this:

| Language | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Old Norse** | **norðr** | north, northward |
| **Old Norse** | **norrœnn** | northern; Norwegian/Norse |
| **Old English** | **norð** | north |
| **Old Saxon** | **north** | north |
| **Dutch** | **noord** | north |
| **German** | **Nord** | north |

These forms descend from a reconstructed Proto-Germanic form roughly ***nurþa- / *nurtha-**, meaning **north**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

So you can basically understand:

**NORSE → NOORSCH → NOORD + -SCH → “northern”**

and Old Norse itself had:

**NORÐR = NORTH**

**NORRŒNN = NORTHERN / NORSE**

There is also a nice connection with **Norway**. Old Norse **Norvegr** meant approximately **“north-way”**:

**norðr** = north  
**vegr** = way, road, route

So **Norvegr → Norway = “the northern way/route.”** :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

Therefore the core **NOR-/NORD-/NORÐ-** idea in Norse, German, Dutch, and English is essentially **NORTH / NORTHERN**. It was originally a directional/geographical term, not the name of a religion or ethnicity.
