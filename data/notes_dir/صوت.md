The English word **shout** and Arabic **صَوْت (ṣawt)** show a striking similarity in both **sound and meaning**. Arabic *ṣawt* is pronounced approximately **“sawt”** /sˤawt/ and means **sound or voice**; related Arabic forms such as **ṣawwata** can mean **to yell or cry out**.

English **shout** appears around 1300 as Middle English **shouten / schowten**, meaning “to call or cry out loudly.” Importantly, its deeper origin is listed as **unknown**, with proposed Germanic explanations remaining speculative.

The resemblance is therefore notable:

**ṣawt** /sˤawt/ — voice, sound  
**shout** /ʃaʊt/ — a loud voice or cry

Both preserve the very distinctive **-awt / -out** sound pattern and belong to essentially the same semantic field of **voice, sound, and loud vocal expression**. A shift from Arabic emphatic **ṣ** to European **s/sh** would also not be inherently impossible in borrowing between languages that lack the Arabic emphatic consonant.

This does **not yet prove** that *shout* was borrowed from Arabic *ṣawt*, because no documented medieval borrowing chain has been established. But because the accepted origin of *shout* itself remains uncertain, the close phonetic and semantic correspondence makes **ṣawt → shout** a legitimate hypothesis worth investigating rather than dismissing as mere coincidence.
