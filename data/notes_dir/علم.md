# علم

Alim of alimentary (soul food),knowing; knowledge; teaching; learning; landmarks; worlds; mountains

 **alimentary** and **alimony** ultimately come from the same Latin verb, but they were formed with **different Latin endings**.

**Alimentary** comes from Latin **alimentum** = “food, nourishment,” from **alere** = “to nourish, feed, raise.” Then **alimentarius** meant “relating to nourishment,” which gave English **alimentary**. 

**Alimony** comes from Latin **alimonia** = “nourishment, sustenance, support,” also from **alere**. The ending **-monia** expresses a state, condition, or provision/support. 

So the breakdown is roughly:

- **alere** = nourish
- **alimentum** = nourishment/food
- **alimentarius** → **alimentary** = relating to nourishment
- **alimonia** → **alimony** = sustenance/support

In **modern German**, **Alim** is not a native German word with an old Germanic meaning. When used, it is generally the borrowed Arabic **ʿālim (عالم)**, meaning a **learned scholar**, especially an Islamic religious scholar.

In **Old Norse**, **alim** really does occur, but it is a **verb form**, not a noun meaning “scholar.” It is the **first-person plural present subjunctive of _ala_**, a verb meaning **to give birth, rear, raise, nourish**. So **alim** would mean approximately:

**“that we may raise / nourish / bring up.”** 

There is also an Old Norse personal name **Álim**, recorded as a shortening of **Áleifr / Óláfr**. 

So the striking comparison is:

**Arabic عالِم ʿālim** → “knowing person, scholar”  
**Old Norse alim** → “we may raise/nourish”  
**German Alim** → normally a borrowing from Arabic, not an inherited Germanic word.
