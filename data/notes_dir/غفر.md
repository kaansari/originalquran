# غفر

gefera, fare, companion, comrade, partner; literally a “fellow traveler



| Language | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Old English** | **ġefēra / gefera** | companion, comrade, partner; literally a “fellow traveler”|
| **Old English** | **ġefar** | an imperative form of **ġefaran**, “to go/travel” |
| **Early New High German** | **gefar / gefär** | danger, threat; earlier also hostile intent, deceit, Jaffar  جَعْفَر|
| **Old Norse** | **gifr-** | savage, fierce, excessive; e.g. *gifrliga*, “savagely” Jaffar جَعْفَر|

### The most interesting match: **Old English gefera**

**ġefēra** was pronounced roughly **ye-FEH-ra / ge-FERA** and meant **companion, comrade, partner**. Its structure was *ge-* + a word connected with **faran**, “to travel/go,” so the literal idea is roughly **“one who goes together with another.”** 

It survives indirectly in archaic English **fere**, meaning **companion, mate, spouse**. 

So purely phonetically:

**GAFARA / GEFERA**

is a remarkably close sequence:

**G-F-R**

and even the full three-syllable pattern is close.

There is also German **Gefährte**, “companion,” which belongs to the same Germanic family as Old English **ġefēra**.
