Yes — there is a **very interesting semantic parallel** between Arabic **ج ز ي (J-Z-Y)** and a Germanic family in Old Norse, German, and English. But based on standard historical linguistics, I would call it a **parallel**, not a proven cognate relationship.

| Language | Word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Arabic** | **جَزَى / jazā** | recompense, repay, give something in return |
| **Old Norse** | **gjalda** | repay, return, pay |
| **Old English** | **gieldan / geldan** | pay, reward, render |
| **English** | **yield** | originally pay/render; later give, produce, surrender |

### Arabic **ج ز ي**

Using **Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lugha**, the root is:

> **الجيم والزاء والياء: قيام الشيء مقام غيره ومكافأته إياه**

Meaning roughly:

**“One thing standing in place of another and matching/recompensing it.”**

He gives **جزيت فلانًا جزاءً** — to recompense someone — and connects it with **قضاء**, fulfillment or settlement. [Islamweb](https://www.islamweb.net/ar/library/content/124/668/?idfrom=\&idto=\&start=\&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So the central Arabic concept is:

**something given in return ↔ equivalent recompense ↔ settling what is due.**

### Old Norse **gjalda**

This is remarkably close semantically.

Old Norse **gjalda** means:

**“to repay, return, pay.”**

A traditional example is:

**gjalda gjǫf við gjǫf**  
= **repay gift with gift.**

That is almost exactly the conceptual field of Arabic **جزاء**: something returned corresponding to what was given. [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/gjalda?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### German

Old High German **geltan** meant:

**repay, pay, compensate, requite.**

Modern German **gelten** shifted mainly toward **“to be worth / count / be valid,”** while **Geld** became the ordinary word for **money**. [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/An_Etymological_Dictionary_of_the_German_Language/Annotated/gelten?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Notice especially the older meaning:

**geltan = requite / compensate**

which is extremely close to **جازى / جزى**.

### English

English **yield** comes from Old English:

**gieldan / geldan**

which originally meant:

**“pay, pay for, reward, render.”**

Its Proto-Germanic ancestor is reconstructed as ***geldaną**, “to pay.” [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/yield?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So historically:

**gieldan → yield**

The modern meaning “produce” developed later from the idea of **giving/rendering something in return**.

### The striking common idea

The semantic structure is unusually close:

**Arabic**

**جَزَى**
→ give something corresponding to an action  
→ recompense  
→ reward/pay back  
→ settle something due

**Germanic**

**gjalda / geldan**
→ give something back  
→ repay  
→ compensate  
→ render/payment

So conceptually:

> **action → corresponding return**

exists on **both sides**.

There is even a stronger connection in English **geld**, historically meaning **payment, tax, tribute, compensation**. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/geld?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### But are جزي and gjalda actually related?

There is **no accepted etymological derivation connecting Arabic J-Z-Y with Germanic *geldaną**.

Germanic **gjalda / geldan / yield / gelten** is reconstructed internally as:

**Proto-Germanic *geldaną**  
→ “pay”

and commonly connected to PIE ***gʰeldʰ-**, “pay.” [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/yield?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Arabic **ج ز ي**, meanwhile, belongs to the Semitic Arabic root system.

So I would rate the comparison:

**Meaning similarity: ★★★★★ very strong**  
**Sound similarity: ★★☆☆☆ weak/moderate**  
**Proven historical relationship: ☆☆☆☆☆ none established**

The most interesting comparison is therefore not really **JZY ≈ GELD** phonetically, but:

> **جَزَى jazā = repay/recompense**  
> **gjalda = repay/return**  
> **geltan = repay/compensate**  
> **gieldan/yield = pay/render**
