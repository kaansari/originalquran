# صبر

Sober,patience; steadfastness
The Arabic **صَبْر (ṣabr)** and English **sober** show a striking resemblance in both **sound structure and meaning**.

Arabic **ṣabr** is built on the consonants **Ṣ-B-R** and expresses ideas such as **restraint, patience, endurance, self-control, and holding oneself back from impulsive action**. A **ṣābir** is therefore someone who remains controlled and does not react hastily.

Older forms of English **sober** were **sobre, sobor, and sobur**, giving essentially the same consonantal framework:

**Ṣ-B-R → S-B-R**

The semantic correspondence is equally notable. Historically, **sober** did not mean only “not drunk.” It also carried meanings such as **temperate, restrained, moderate, calm, sensible, and self-controlled**. Old English **sýfre** likewise described someone who was **temperate, restrained, and not ruled by appetite or passion**.

So the comparison can be summarized:

**ṣabr** — restraint, endurance, patience, self-control  
**ṣābir** — a restrained, patient person  
**sobre / sober** — restrained, moderate, calm, self-controlled

This is more than a superficial similarity: both words preserve an **S-B-R-type consonantal pattern** and center around essentially the same human quality—**controlling oneself rather than acting impulsively or hastily**.

The conventional etymology traces English *sober* through Old French **sobre** to Latin **sōbrius**, so a direct Arabic origin is not currently established. But the combination of **close consonantal structure and unusually close meaning** makes **ṣabr–sober** an interesting linguistic hypothesis that deserves comparison rather than being dismissed simply because the standard dictionaries give a Latin transmission history.
