Earth

**Earth** is a very old Germanic word.

The development is:

**Modern English _earth_**  
← Middle English **erthe**  
← Old English **eorþe** = “ground, soil, dirt, dry land; the world”  
← Proto-Germanic **\*erþō / \*erthō** = “earth, ground.” [Etymology Online](https://www.etymonline.com/word/earth?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Its close relatives are:

| Language | Word |
|---|---|
| Old English | **eorþe** |
| Old Saxon | **ertha** |
| Old High German | **erda** |
| German | **Erde** |
| Gothic | **airþa** |
| Old Norse | **jörð** |
| Dutch | **aarde** |

All belong to the same Germanic family. [Etymology Online](https://www.etymonline.com/word/earth?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The important sound pattern is therefore roughly:

**ERTH / ERD / JORD**

The **th** in English **earth** corresponds historically to **d** in forms such as German **Erde**, while Old Norse developed the word into **jörð**. [Etymology Online](https://www.etymonline.com/word/earth?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Its deeper Indo-European origin is less certain. It is often reconstructed from a PIE earth/ground word such as **\*h₁érteh₂**, while another analysis connects it with a root **\*er-**, “earth, ground.” [Etymology Online](https://www.etymonline.com/word/earth?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

One interesting comparison given by historical dictionaries is that Arabic **أَرْض (ʾarḍ)** and Hebrew **אֶרֶץ (ʾereṣ)** sound strikingly similar to Germanic **earth / erda**, but mainstream historical linguistics considers the Semitic and Germanic words **probably unrelated rather than proven cognates**.
